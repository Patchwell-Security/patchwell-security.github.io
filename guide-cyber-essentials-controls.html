The 5 Cyber Essentials controls in plain English

What the scheme actually asks of a small business, and a quick check for each part. About 15 minutes to read.

Based on the NCSC's Cyber Essentials: Requirements for IT Infrastructure v3.3 (April 2026), the version used for new assessments since 27 April 2026. Checked on 23 November 2026.

What is Cyber Essentials?

Cyber Essentials is the UK government-backed scheme that shows your business has the basic protections in place against the most common online attacks. It's run by the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) with its delivery partner, IASME.

  • Who asks for it: many public-sector contracts, larger customers checking their suppliers, and some insurers.
  • Cost: the basic certificate is priced by business size, starting at £320 + VAT for micro businesses (0–9 employees). Check IASME's site for the current price.
  • A bonus: UK organisations with turnover under £20m that certify their whole organisation get cyber liability insurance included, through IASME (terms apply).
  • Two levels: Cyber Essentials is a self-assessment checked by an assessor. Cyber Essentials Plus covers the same five controls but adds a hands-on technical test of your systems.
Two rules you cannot fail since April 2026. Under the current "Danzell" question set, getting either of these wrong fails the whole assessment:
  1. Two-step sign-in (MFA) on every cloud service that offers it, such as email, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, accounting software and online banking.
  2. High-risk and critical updates installed within 14 days of release.
Cloud services can also no longer be left out of your assessment.

The five controls

  1. Firewalls

    In plain English: a filter between the internet and your devices, so only the traffic you expect gets in.

    What you need:

    • A working firewall protecting every device: usually your broadband router, plus the firewall built into each laptop.
    • The router's default admin password changed to a strong, unique one.
    • The router's settings page not reachable from the internet, unless you have a written reason and extra protection.
    • No "open doors" (port forwarding) unless someone has approved and written down why. Remove any you no longer need.
    • The laptop's own firewall switched on, especially when it's used on public Wi-Fi.

    Quick check: follow our router guide for BT, Sky or Virgin Media. That covers most of this control.

  2. Secure configuration

    In plain English: set devices up so there are as few ways in as possible.

    What you need:

    • Unused accounts removed or disabled, such as guest accounts or old staff logins.
    • Every default or easy-to-guess password changed.
    • Software and apps you don't use uninstalled.
    • Auto-run switched off, so a USB stick can't run files by itself.
    • Devices lock when you walk away, and need a PIN, password or fingerprint to unlock.
    • Guessing is limited: no more than 10 wrong attempts before the device locks or slows down. A PIN that only unlocks the device must be at least 6 characters.

    Quick check: on each laptop and phone, look at the list of apps and the list of user accounts. Anything you don't recognise or use should go.

  3. Security update management

    In plain English: keep everything updated, because most attacks use holes that already have a fix.

    What you need:

    • All software licensed and still supported by its maker. Old versions that no longer get updates must be removed, or the device kept completely off the internet.
    • Automatic updates switched on wherever possible.
    • Critical or high-risk updates installed within 14 days of release. This also applies when the maker doesn't say how serious an update is.

    Quick check: are any of your computers running a version of Windows or macOS that no longer gets updates? That's the most common reason small businesses fail.

  4. User access control

    In plain English: everyone has their own login, only gets access to what they need, and accounts are properly protected.

    What you need:

    • Everyone has their own account. No shared logins.
    • Accounts are removed or disabled when someone leaves or no longer needs them.
    • Two-step sign-in (MFA) on every cloud service that offers it.
    • A separate admin account for installing software and changing settings, which is never used for email or browsing.
    • Strong passwords, using one of three options: MFA; or at least 12 characters; or at least 8 characters with common passwords blocked. Never a maximum length.
    • Passwords changed straight away if you think they've leaked. Passkeys and other passwordless sign-ins are accepted too.

    Quick check: list every online service your business uses, from email to accounting to banking. Is two-step sign-in switched on for all of them?

  5. Malware protection

    In plain English: stop viruses and ransomware from running.

    What you need, using at least one of these:

    • Anti-malware software on Windows and Mac computers, kept up to date, which blocks malware from running and blocks known dangerous websites.
    • Application allow listing: only approved, properly signed apps can run, and users can't install unsigned ones. Phones and tablets that only install apps from the official app store generally work this way.

    Quick check: open the security settings on each computer and confirm its virus protection is switched on and recently updated.

Not a requirement, but don't skip it

The 2026 version stresses the importance of backups, even though they aren't a technical requirement. If ransomware does get in, a recent backup kept separately from your main computer is what gets you back to work.

How to apply

  1. Look through the free self-assessment questions on IASME's website first. You can download them before paying anything.
  2. Fix anything you can't answer "yes" to.
  3. Register and pay through IASME, then answer the questions online.
  4. A board member or equivalent (for a sole trader, that's you) signs off the answers, and an assessor marks them.

Your quick checklist

  • Router secured and its default password changed
  • Unused accounts, apps and default passwords removed
  • Every device and program still supported and set to update automatically
  • Critical and high-risk updates installed within 14 days
  • Two-step sign-in on every cloud service that offers it
  • Separate admin account, never used for email or browsing
  • Malware protection switched on and updating on every computer
  • Regular backups kept somewhere separate
